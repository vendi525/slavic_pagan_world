## The name

Lucija comes from the Latin *lux*, light. In the old Julian calendar, December 13 fell right beside the winter solstice, so Lucija night was one of the longest nights of the year. Kropej writes that the day was, by that calendar, "the beginning of winter."[1]

A night named after light, on the darkest edge of the year. That is the whole spell of Lucija.

## The white woman of midwinter

Long before there was a Saint Lucy, the Slavs and their neighbors knew a mighty female spirit of midwinter. She was the one who spun, who bred, who brought light and who guarded the rules of women's work. In some places she was called Pehtra Baba. When Christianity arrived, "Pehtra was replaced by St. Lucia, who brings light" (Kropej).[2]

The saint took her name, but the old woman kept walking. On her night, the Lucias went from house to house. They would "threaten children to put out their eyes but occasionally they also bring them gifts." And "like Pehtra Baba, Lucia brings light, helping the sun to shine more brightly during the dark and cold winter months" (Kropej).[1]

Light and fear in the same hand. Lucija is not a gentle candle saint. She is winter itself at the door: beautiful, strict, and impossible to ignore.

## The forbidden work

Lucija's day opened one of the sacred quarter weeks of the old year, the Ember days, when ordinary life had to stop. During this time, people "were forbidden to go courting, do female chores, bake bread, and leave their house on Saturday nights" (Kropej).[3]

Those who disobeyed and wandered out in the dark would meet the night women in their true shape: shrieking monsters with tousled hair. And the women who kept spinning when they should have rested would get a visit they never forgot.

It is the same rule as {% include wiki-link.html slug="mokosh" text="Mokosh's Friday" %}. Even the busiest hands must stop when the year turns.

## Lucija's stool and the witches

The most mysterious custom of Lucija night is the stool. Across Eastern Europe, people believed you could unmask witches with it. "This is a footstool whose construction had to begin on St. Lucia's day (13th December) and it had to be completed on Christmas Eve or Christmas day" (Kropej).[4]

It had to be made from nine, twelve or thirteen kinds of wood, a new one each day, and often in complete silence. Whoever brought it to church on Christmas and stood on it would see the witches of the village for who they truly were.

Twelve days of silent work in the dark, for one moment of sight. Lucija gives light, but you have to earn it.

## Lucija's wheat

In many homes across Southeast Europe, wheat is still sown in a small dish on Lucija's day. By Christmas it stands green and tall, a little field growing in the middle of winter. If it grows thick, the next harvest will be good.

In the darkest days of the year, people planted proof that life comes back.

## Questions about Lucija night

### What is Lucija night?

Lucija night (St. Lucy's night) is December 13, the old beginning of winter. In folklore it is the night of Lucija, a midwinter spirit who brings light but also punishes and frightens.

### Is St. Lucy's Day pagan?

St. Lucy is a Christian saint, but in Slavic and Southeast European folklore she took over the role of an older midwinter goddess, Pehtra Baba, who brought light and guarded women's work.

### Why was December 13 the longest night?

In the old Julian calendar, December 13 fell close to the winter solstice, so Lucija night was one of the longest nights of the year.

### What is Lucija's stool?

A stool built from many kinds of wood, started on Lucija's day and finished at Christmas. People believed whoever stood on it in church could see the witches.
