Before the thunder and before the young sun, there was the sky itself: wide, bright and very old. The Slavs called it Svarog.

## The name

Svarog's name is usually linked to old words for shining and for the sky. Russian scholars compared it with the Sanskrit *svar*, "and the later word Svarga, heaven" (Ralston).[1] Others heard in it the Slavic *svariti*, to weld or forge. Both fit. Svarog is the sky, and he is also the smith who works in it.

## The father who rested

Svarog is the oldest of the Slavic gods, and the quietest. He made the world and then stepped back. "The Sun and the Fire are spoken of as his children; the former under the name of Dazhbog, the latter under that of Ogon'. According to an old saying, Svarog is given to repose, deputing to his children the work of creation and the task of ruling the universe" (Ralston).[1]

So his children did the work. The Sun crossed the sky every day. The Fire lived on every hearth. A 13th-century writer noted that the Slavs still "pray to Ogon', whom they call Svarozhich," Svarog's son (Ralston).[2] Every flame in the stove was a little piece of the sky god, brought down to earth.

## The smith who set the sun in heaven

In a Russian chronicle from the 1100s, a monk translating a Greek history book came across Hephaistos, the Greek blacksmith god, and wrote the name Svarog in his place. The same text gives Svarog a law: he ordered that every man should have one wife and every woman one husband, and that whoever broke this "should be cast into a fiery furnace" (Máchal).[3]

The Baltic neighbours of the Slavs sang of a smith with his forge in the sky. The old chronicle says that he "forged for him (Perkunas) the sun as it shines on earth, and set the sun in heaven" (Máchal).[4]

Picture the forge at dawn. The bellows breathe, the coals glow red, and on the anvil, under the hammer, the sun takes shape. Then the smith lifts it with his tongs and hangs it in the sky. The sparks that fly off are the stars.

## How Svarog survived

Svarog himself faded early, but his son did not. Among the Elbe Slavs, in the temple at Radigast, the most important idol was Svarožič, and "each of them had his name carved on his image" (Máchal).[5] In the south, the "little Svarog" became {% include wiki-link.html slug="bozic" text="Božič" %}, the young sun born at the solstice, and his name became the word for Christmas.

And his fire stayed in every house. The worship of Ogon, the fire, went on as respect for the hearth, the stove and the {% include wiki-link.html slug="koleda" text="Christmas log" %}. In one Russian province it was still forbidden to break up the smouldering logs in the stove with a poker, since that might make the family's "ancestors" fall through (Ralston).[6]

## What is old and what is new

Old: Svarog's name in a 12th-century Russian chronicle; Svarožič as the god of fire, worshipped by Russians and by the Elbe Slavs (11th century); the Baltic heavenly smith.

New: the idea of Svarog as the supreme creator god and "Kolo Svaroga", the wheel of Svarog as a pagan calendar, come from modern Slavic native faith of the 20th century. They are beautiful ideas, but the old texts don't contain them.

## Questions about Svarog

### Who is Svarog in Slavic mythology?

Svarog is the old Slavic god of the sky and the heavenly forge. His children are the Sun (Dazhbog) and Fire (Svarožič), who rule the world while he rests.

### Is Svarog the god of fire?

Svarog is the sky and the smith. Fire is his son, Svarožič. But since the smith works with fire, the two are closely linked.

### What is Kolo Svaroga?

Kolo Svaroga, "the wheel of Svarog", is a modern name for the Slavic wheel of the year, the cycle of seasons and holidays. The name comes from 20th-century Slavic native faith, not from old sources.

### Is Svarog the same as Hephaestus?

A medieval Russian translator used Svarog's name for the Greek smith god Hephaestus, so they were seen as alike. Svarog was also a sky god, which Hephaestus was not.
