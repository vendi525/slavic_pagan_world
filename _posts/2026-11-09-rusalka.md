On a warm night in early summer, the river is quiet, but the mill wheel is turning. Look closer and you'll see a girl clinging to it, going round and round in the splash, her long green hair streaming behind her. She is a Rusalka.

## The name

Her name probably comes from water. It "appears to be connected with rus, an old Slavonic word for a stream, or with ruslo, the bed of a river," and with *rosa*, dew (Ralston).[1] Other scholars link it to the Roman spring feast of roses, the Rosalia, which became the Slavic Rusalia, the week of the dead in early summer.

## The maidens of the river

The Rusalki are "beauteous maidens," with pale skin and wild eyes, and "their hair is long and thick and wavy, and green as is the grass" (Ralston).[1] They sit on the banks combing their hair, or "cling to a mill-wheel, and turn round with it amid the splash of the stream." If anyone comes near, they splash into the water and call him to join them. "Whomsoever they get hold of they tickle to death" (Ralston).[1]

Their life is in their hair. "If a Rusalka's hair becomes dry she dies," so she never goes far from the water without her comb. And "so long as she has a comb she can always produce a flood by passing it through her waving locks" (Ralston).[2]

Who were they? The old answer is sad. The Rusalki were the souls of girls who had drowned, and of babies who died before they could be named or baptised. The little ones look like seven-year-old girls, and for seven years they fly about at Pentecost, asking to be christened (Ralston).[5]

## Rusalka Week

In winter the Rusalki disappear. They come back with the green. In the week before Pentecost they leave the water to sit in the trees, and "as many songs testify, they sit upon trees, and ask for linen garments" (Ralston).[2] So women hung shirts, rags and skeins of thread on the oak branches for them.

During the Green Week "no one dares to work for fear of offending the Rusalkas," above all women must not sew or wash linen (Ralston).[3] Girls went into the woods and threw garlands to the Rusalki, "asking for rich husbands in return, or float them down a stream," reading their future in how the wreaths drifted (Ralston).[3]

The Rusalki also had power over the harvest. On the eve of Pentecost they ran into the fields and swung on the stalks of rye, "so that the corn undulates as if beneath a strong wind" (Ralston).[4]

Picture the fields in June: knee-high green rye, the wind running through it in waves. It's not the wind. It's the Rusalki, laughing.

## Sending the Rusalka away

At the end of Rusalka Week the village saw her off. A straw figure was dressed as a woman to stand for the Rusalka, and the young people danced it out to the fields. There they split into two sides, one attacking the doll and one defending it, until "eventually it is torn to pieces, and the straw of which it was made is thrown to the winds, after which the performers return home, saying they have expelled the Rusalka" (Ralston).[4]

It's the same old gesture as drowning Morana in spring. Something that belongs to the other world is welcomed, fed and then firmly sent back.

## What is old and what is new

Old: the Rusalka week of the dead, the offerings on trees, the wreaths on the water and the expelling of the straw Rusalka, recorded across Russia, Ukraine and Belarus in the 1800s.

Newer: the Rusalka as a fish-tailed mermaid comes from Western art and from Pushkin and Dvořák's opera *Rusalka* (1901). In the old beliefs she has legs and dances in the rye.

## Questions about the Rusalka

### What is a Rusalka?

A Rusalka is a Slavic water spirit, usually the soul of a girl who drowned or died young. She lives in rivers and lakes and comes out in early summer.

### What does a Rusalka look like?

A pale, beautiful girl with long green hair, dressed in leaves or a long white shift. In some regions she is described as ugly and hairy instead.

### What is Rusalka Week?

Rusalka Week, or Green Week, is the week around Pentecost in early summer. The Rusalki leave the water, people avoid work and swimming, hang gifts on trees and float wreaths.

### Is a Rusalka a mermaid?

Not in the old sense. The folk Rusalka has legs, sits in trees and dances in fields. The fish-tailed Rusalka came later, from literature and opera.
