High on the hill, where the oaks grow and the lightning strikes first, lived Perun: the god of thunder, the keeper of oaths, the one the Slavs looked up to when the sky turned black.

## The name

Perun means "the striker". The same old root that gives the Polish word for a thunderbolt, *piorun*, also gives the Lithuanian thunder god Perkūnas. Wherever his name survived, it meant the crack of lightning hitting the ground. Slovenes also called him Gromovnik, the Thunderer.

## The oldest written god

Perun is the first Slavic god anyone ever wrote about. In the 500s, a Byzantine historian watched the Slavs from across the Danube and noted what they believed:

> "For they believe that one god, the maker of the lightning, is alone lord of all things, and they sacrifice to him cattle."
>
> <cite>Procopius[1]</cite>

He doesn't give the god a name. He doesn't need to. Four hundred years later, in Kyiv, the same god has a name and a face. Prince Vladimir set up idols on the hill above his hall, and the first of them was Perun, "made of wood with a head of silver and a mustache of gold" (Primary Chronicle).[2] Beside him stood Khors, Dazhbog, Stribog, Simargl and {% include wiki-link.html slug="mokosh" text="Mokosh" %}.

## The sky against the deep

Perun's great enemy was Veles, the dragon of the underworld. Their fight is the oldest story the Slavs told. In Slovenia, people still remembered "the fight between the heavenly deity and the chthonic god", and how Perun "uses his golden axe or his hammer, a symbol of thunder, to strike at the hostile snake" (Kropej).[4]

Picture a summer afternoon. The air goes heavy and still. Somewhere in the valley, Veles has stolen the cattle, or the rain, and hidden them under the rock. Then the first rumble rolls over the hills. Every thunderclap is Perun's axe coming down. Every lightning bolt is aimed at the snake. When the storm breaks, the rain falls, the fields drink, and the world is put back in order.

## The fall of Perun

When Vladimir chose Christianity in 988, Perun was the first to go. The prince "ordered that Perun should be bound to a horse's tail and dragged down Borichev to the stream", and twelve men beat the idol with sticks on the way (Primary Chronicle).[3] Those who had not yet been baptised wept as their god was dragged past. Then the idol was thrown into the Dnieper. Below the rapids the wind threw it ashore, at a spot that was called Perun's sandbank ever after.

## How Perun survived

You can't drown a thunderstorm. The god's name stayed in people's mouths as a curse. A Belarusian peasant in a temper would still shout "Perun smite thee!", and the Slovaks kept the curse "May Parom show thee his teeth!", meaning, may the lightning strike you (Ralston).[5]

The rest of Perun went to a saint. In Russia his "attributes have been transferred to the Prophet Elijah" (Ralston),[6] who rides across the sky in a fiery chariot and makes the thunder. St Elijah's Day, 20 July, at the height of the summer storms, became Perun's day in all but name. In the Balkans it is still called Ilinden, and people still say that when it thunders, Elijah is driving overhead.

## What is old and what is new

Old: a Slavic lightning god who was lord over all (Procopius, 6th century); oaths sworn by Perun and his idol in Kyiv (Primary Chronicle, 10th century); curses using his name.

Later: the full story of Perun fighting Veles for the stolen cattle was pieced together by 20th-century scholars from folk songs and tales across the Slavic world. Thursday as Perun's day comes from comparison with Thor's day.

## Questions about Perun

### Who is Perun in Slavic mythology?

Perun is the Slavic god of thunder and lightning, the ruler of the sky and the eternal enemy of Veles, the god of the underworld. He was the chief god of Prince Vladimir's pantheon in Kyiv.

### What is Perun's weapon?

Perun fights with an axe or a hammer, the symbol of thunder. His lightning bolts are sometimes called his arrows.

### Is Perun the same as Thor?

They are cousins, not the same god. Perun, Thor, the Lithuanian Perkūnas and the Vedic Indra all come from an old Indo-European storm god who fights a serpent.

### What happened to Perun?

In 988, Prince Vladimir had Perun's idol dragged to the Dnieper and thrown in the river. His thunder later passed to St Elijah, whose day is 20 July.
