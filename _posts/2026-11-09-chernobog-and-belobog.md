## The names

The names are as plain as names get. *Cherno-bog* is the black god, *Belo-bog* the white god. The priest Helmold, who lived among the Baltic Slavs in the 1100s, wrote the first one down and translated it for his readers: "Zcerneboch, id est nigrum deum", Zcerneboch, that is, the black god (Helmold).[1]

White here means more than a colour. In old Russian the word for white "originally meant bright," and people still spoke of the "white" light or the "white" day (Ralston).[3] Belobog is the bright god, the god of daylight.

## The black god at the feast

Helmold's report is short. At their feasts the Slavs passed a cup around and spoke over it in the names of two gods, a good one and a bad one. They believed "that happiness comes from the god of good, while misfortune is dispensed by the deity of evil," and the evil one they called Chernobog (Máchal).[2]

So the Black God was not prayed to for anything. He was named so that he would stay away: from the harvest, from the cattle, from the children asleep upstairs. You speak to him the way you shut a door against the wind.

Belobog, strangely, is never named in that chronicle. Only his shadow is. But if there was a black god at the feast, people reasoned, there was a white one beside him, and the land itself seems to agree.

## Two hills, a white one and a black one

All over the Slavic world the two gods were left behind in place names. There is Belye Bogi, "White Gods", near Moscow, and among the Lusatian Sorbs two mountains called Beli Bog and Černi Bog. "The first is perceived as a positive location, while the last denotes a negative one" (Kropej).[5] In Russia there was a Byelbozhsky monastery near Kostroma, and a place called Chernobozh'e near Porkhov (Ralston).[3]

Picture it: two hills facing each other across a valley, one where you go up with flowers, one you walk around.

## The old man in white

Belobog did not disappear. In Belarus he became Byelun, "an old man with a long white beard, dressed in white, and carrying a staff in his hand, who appears only by day, and who assists travellers to find their way out of the dark forests" (Ralston).[3] He is "the bestower of wealth and fertility," and at harvest he comes into the fields to help the reapers (Ralston).[3]

The peasants had sayings about him. "Dark is it in the forest without Byelun," they said, and of a lucky man: "He must have made friends with Byelun" (Ralston).[4]

The Black God lived on too, in curses. In Ukraine people still said "May the black god smite thee!" (Ralston).[4]

## What is old and what is new

Old: Chernobog is in a 12th-century chronicle of the Baltic Slavs, named over the feast cup as the god of misfortune. The white and black god names survive in hills, villages and monasteries, and the White God lives on as Byelun in Belarusian folklore.

Newer: Belobog's name is not in any old text. He was pieced together from place names and folklore. The idea of a cosmic war between light and darkness is a modern picture, and the Black God as pure evil was already shaped by Christian ideas of the devil, as Máchal noted (Máchal).[2]

## Questions about Chernobog and Belobog

### Who is Chernobog?

Chernobog, the Black God, is the Slavic god of misfortune. A 12th-century chronicle says the Baltic Slavs named him at feasts, alongside a good god, to keep bad luck away.

### Who is Belobog?

Belobog, the White God, is the bright god of good luck and daylight. His name survives in place names, and in Belarus he lives on as Byelun, an old man in white who helps lost travellers.

### Is Chernobog the devil?

Not originally. He was the god who sends misfortune. Over time, Christian ideas turned him into something like the devil.

### Were Chernobog and Belobog brothers?

No old source says so. They are a pair of opposites, black and white, bad and good luck, but their family story is a modern invention.
