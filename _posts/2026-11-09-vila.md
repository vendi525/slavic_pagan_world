## The name

The Vila is the oldest fairy the Slavs had. In the 500s, Procopius already wrote that the Slavs worshipped beings like the Greek nymphs and offered them sacrifices (Máchal).[1] Her name may come from an old root meaning "to perish", related to the Lithuanian *vėlės*, "spirits of the deceased" (Máchal).[1] Many people believed the Vile were the souls of girls who had died young: in Slovakia, "souls of brides who died after their betrothal" who could not rest (Máchal).[1]

## The fairy who left

The Vile once lived side by side with people. In the good old days, "the fairies helped them to garner their harvests, to mow their grass, to feed their cattle, and to build their houses; they taught them how to plough, to sow" (Máchal).[2]

Then people changed. The shepherds threw away their flutes and took up whips, cursing in the pastures, and "when, finally, the first reports of guns were heard, and nations began to make war against each other, the Vily left the country" (Máchal).[2] Now they can be seen only rarely, sitting on a bare rock and singing sad songs.

They are pictured as "beautiful women, eternally young, with pale cheeks, and dressed in white," with golden hair, bodies "as slender as the stem of a pine," and voices "so fine and sweet that to hear them once is to remember them forever" (Máchal).[3]

## The hero's sister

Every honest man, people said, had a Vila for a sister, who helped him when he was in danger.[3] The greatest South Slavic hero, Marko, got his strength from one. As a weak little swineherd beaten by the other boys, he ran away into the fields and met a Vila. She told him to drink from her breast and then lift a twelve-hundredweight stone. He drank and tried, again and again, until "he was already able to cast it to a great height and over hills, so that it was no more to be found" (Wratislaw).[4]

## The wife who must not be named

The most beautiful Vila tale comes from Istria. A young man walking over Mount Učka on a hot day found a maiden in white asleep on the grass. Not wanting to wake her, he broke off a branch and planted it beside her for shade. When she woke, she agreed to marry him, with one condition: "you must never utter my name; if you speak my name Vila, I must quit you at once" (Wratislaw).[5]

They lived happily for years and had a daughter. Then one summer a hailstorm came before the wheat was cut. The wife only smiled and stepped outside, and when the hail stopped, the husband found all his wheat safe in sheaves on the threshing floor. Amazed, he cried out: "Ah, she is a Vila! she is a Vila!" And in that moment she vanished. She still came back, "visible only to her little daughter, helping her in all needs, as the most careful mother" (Wratislaw).[5]

## What is old and what is new

Old: Procopius's nymphs (6th century), and the Vile in medieval South Slavic texts and in the hero songs about Marko. The tales here were written down in the 1800s.

Newer: the ballet *Giselle* (1841) made the Polish and Czech "Wili" famous as ghostly dancing brides. Modern fantasy turned the Vila into a seductive spirit, but in the old tales she is first a sister, a helper and a mother.

## Questions about the Vila

### What is a Vila in Slavic mythology?

A Vila is a Slavic fairy who lives in the clouds, in the mountains or by springs and lakes. She is beautiful, strong and wise, and can help or punish people.

### What does a Vila look like?

A young woman in white with long golden hair and a pale face. She can have wings and can turn into a swan, a falcon, a horse or a snake.

### Are Vile good or evil?

Both. They help heroes, protect crops and heal the sick, but they punish anyone who steps into their dance, drinks from their springs without asking or insults them.

### What is the difference between a Vila and a Rusalka?

The Vila is more of a mountain and cloud fairy, mostly South Slavic. The Rusalka is more of a water spirit, mostly East Slavic. Their festivals and stories often overlap.
