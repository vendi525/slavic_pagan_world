## The name

The word koleda "is derived from the Latin calendae", the first day of the month, and names "certain days at Christmas and Easter when children go from house to house, singing songs and expecting all sorts of small presents in return" (Máchal).[1]

The carolers who carry the songs are still called koledniki or kolyadniki. Their songs are the kolyadki, and some of them are older than the churches they are sung beside.

## The sun maiden in her sledge

In Russian folk belief, the sun herself was a woman. At the winter solstice she dresses for the feast: she "arrays herself in her holiday robe and head-dress, takes her seat in her telega, and urges her horses upon the summer track" (Ralston).[2]

People called this sun goddess Kolyada. And they brought her to life: a girl "dressed in white, who was seated on a sledge and driven about from house to house," while young people sang the Kolyada songs around her and received presents in return (Ralston).[2]

Imagine a winter village in the dark, snow under the runners, and a girl in white gliding from door to door like the sun returning. Every house she visits is promised a brighter year.

## The log that is welcomed home

Among the South Slavs, the heart of the festival is the badnjak, a log of oak, beech or ash cut in the forest before sunrise. The head of the house carries it inside while the children follow behind him, cheeping like chickens. He lays it by the fire with honey, wine and a bit of every dish, and then "he addresses the log with the words, "Welcome! Come and eat your supper!"" (Máchal).[3]

Then the badnjak goes into the fire. It is not just wood. It "may certainly be regarded as a special symbol of the genius of the house in his capacity of protector of the hearth, which is rekindled on this day" (Máchal).[3]

On the longest night, every home lights its own small sun.

## A supper for the dead

Koleda is a feast of the living, but the dead are invited too. In the East Slavic lands, hay is spread under the tablecloth, the master of the house remembers those who are absent, and "during the meal a portion of the food is set aside for the deceased" (Máchal).[4]

The festive dish is kutiya, a sweet porridge of grain, honey and poppy seeds. It is the same dish eaten at funeral feasts and on All Souls' Day. At the darkest point of the year, the door between the worlds stands open, and the family at the table is bigger than it looks.

## The carolers from the underworld

Why do carolers wear masks and come in the night? Some scholars read the old myth hidden in them. When the young god is born at the turn of the year, it is the carolers, "representing Veles," who take him to "Veles' world of the dead," and in spring he returns to the living (Kropej).[5]

So when the koledniki knock at your door, it might not be only your neighbors. It might be the underworld itself, singing for its supper, and you had better give them a gift.

## Questions about Koleda

### What is Koleda?

Koleda is the Slavic festival of the winter solstice, celebrating the rebirth of the sun with carols, fire, gifts and a festive supper. Today its customs survive in Slavic Christmas traditions.

### What does Koleda mean?

The word comes from the Latin calendae, the first day of the month. It names the midwinter days when carolers go from house to house.

### Who is Kolyada?

In Russian folk belief, Kolyada was the sun, imagined as a woman who rides out on the summer track after the winter solstice. A girl dressed in white represented her in the old processions.

### What is the badnjak?

The badnjak is a log cut on Christmas Eve, welcomed into the house like a guest and burned on the hearth. It stands for the spirit and fire of the home, much like the Yule log.
