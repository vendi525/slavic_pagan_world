Every old Slavic house had one more member of the family than you could see. He lived behind the stove, he never paid rent, and he knew everything that happened under the roof. He was the Domovoi.

## The name

*Dom* means house, so the Domovoi is simply "the one of the house". But people rarely used his name. They called him Grandfather, or the Master, or just "himself". You don't call a member of the family by a title.

## The grandfather in the fire

Long before he was a little hairy man, the Domovoi was the fire itself, and the spirits of the family's dead who sat around it. That is why, when a Russian family moved house, "the fire is raked out of the old stove into a jar and solemnly conveyed to the new one, the words "Welcome, grandfather, to the new home" being uttered when it arrives" (Ralston).[2]

Picture it. A cart piled with benches and bundles, the cow walking behind, and an old woman holding a clay pot of glowing embers in her lap the whole way, like a baby. The house is not a home until grandfather arrives.

He was shy and you were not meant to see him. In winter "the tracks of his shaggy feet may be seen" in the snow, and "his hairy hands are felt by night gliding over the faces of sleepers. When his hand feels soft and warm it is a sign of good luck: when it is cold and bristly, misfortune is to be looked for" (Ralston).[1]

## Master of the house

The Domovoi was "the representative of the housekeeping ideal", "industrious and frugal", and he "watches over the homestead and all that belongs to it" (Ralston).[4] He loved the horses and plaited their manes at night. He kept the forest spirit out of the garden and the witches away from the cows.

He also shared the family's grief. Before a death in the house he could be heard "sighing, weeping, or sitting at his work with his cap pulled over his eyes" (Ralston).[4]

But he had a temper. If he was neglected, he knocked the tables and benches about at night (Ralston).[3] Usually a firm word was enough. When one Domovoi threw the cat on the floor, the housewife scolded him: "Is that the way to manage a house? We can't get on without our cat. A pretty manager, forsooth!" And from then on he left the cat alone (Ralston).[5]

## How he survived

The Domovoi outlived every great god. Long after Perun's idol was thrown into the Dnieper, peasants still set aside a bit of supper for him each night. On 28 January, "after supper, leave out a pot of stewed grain for the Domovoy. This pot is placed on the hearth in front of the stove, and surrounded with hot embers" (Ralston).[2]

## What is old and what is new

Old: the hearth as the place of the family's ancestors, carrying the fire to a new house, and feeding the spirit of the house. These customs are shared with many old European peoples.

Newer: his hairy, devilish look came after Christianity, when the church saw every spirit as a demon. Ralston says so himself.

## Questions about the Domovoi

### What is a Domovoi?

A Domovoi is the Slavic house spirit. He lives behind the stove, protects the family and the animals, and was once thought of as the spirit of the family's ancestors.

### What does a Domovoi look like?

He is usually a small old man, the size of a child, covered in hair, sometimes in a red shirt. He can also take the shape of the master of the house, or of a snake.

### How do you make a Domovoi happy?

Keep the house clean, don't quarrel, and leave him a little food from supper, such as bread, porridge or milk, near the stove. When you move house, invite him to come with you.

### Is a Domovoi good or evil?

A Domovoi is good to his own family but can be mischievous when ignored. To other households he can be a thief.
