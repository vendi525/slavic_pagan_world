## The name

*Baba* means old woman, or grandmother. *Yaga* is harder. It has been linked to old Slavic words for anger, illness and horror. Ralston, who first brought her tales into English, called her "a female fiend whose name has given rise to much philological discussion of a somewhat unsatisfactory nature" (Ralston).[1] In other words, nobody knows, and she likes it that way.

## The hut on hen's legs

Every tale tells it the same way. The hero walks until the forest goes dark, and there is the hut, turning. Baba Yaga is "a tall, gaunt hag, with dishevelled hair," lying "from one corner to the other of a miserable hut, through the ceiling of which passes her long iron nose" (Ralston).[1] The hut faces the forest and shows its back to the road. You have to know the words to make it turn:

> "Hut, hut! stand with thy back to the forest, thy front to me."
>
> <cite>Curtin[3]</cite>

When she goes out "she rides in a mortar, which she urges on with a pestle, while she sweeps away the traces of her flight with a broom." Even "the Day and the Night" are her servants (Ralston).[1]

Picture the yard around the hut: a fence of bones with a skull on every post. In the tale of Vasilisa, when night falls, "the eyes in the skulls glowed" (Afanasyev).[4] This is not a house in the village. It is the last house before the other world.

## The girl who was kind

In the most famous tale, a cruel stepmother sends her stepdaughter to Baba Yaga "for a needle and thread." A good aunt warns the girl what is waiting there and tells her what to do: tie a ribbon on the birch tree, oil the creaking doors, throw bread to the dogs, give the cat some bacon (Ralston).[2]

The girl finds Baba Yaga weaving, and is set to weave too while the witch goes to heat the bath, meaning to eat her. But the cat, the dogs, the doors and the birch tree all let the girl pass, because she was kind to them. When Baba Yaga scolds them, the birch answers that in all its years nobody had ever tied even a thread around it, "but she fastened a ribbon around me." Then Baba Yaga, "bony of limb, quickly jumped into her mortar" and flew after the girl, but too late (Ralston).[2]

## The grandmother who helps

But Baba Yaga is not only a monster. In the tale of Bright Finist the Falcon, a girl wears out three pairs of iron shoes looking for her lost love. At the end of each pair she finds a hut on hen's legs, and inside, a Baba Yaga. Each one sniffs the air, then feeds her, puts her to bed, and in the morning gives her a magic gift for the road. "Now go, with God, to my second sister" (Curtin).[3]

The same old woman who eats the lazy and the rude guides the brave and the patient.

## What is old and what is new

Old: Baba Yaga is in the oldest Russian tale collections of the 1800s, already with her hut, her mortar and her bony legs. Similar forest women appear among Czechs, Poles and Slovaks.

New: the idea that she was once a great goddess, of death, of winter, or of the earth, comes from scholars, not from the tales. Her hut on "chicken legs" in modern books is a softer version of the old "fowl's legs".

## Questions about Baba Yaga

### Who is Baba Yaga?

Baba Yaga is the old witch of Slavic fairy tales. She lives in the forest in a hut on hen's legs, flies in a mortar and is both dangerous and wise.

### Why does Baba Yaga's house have chicken legs?

In the tales the hut stands on hen's or fowl's legs so it can turn around. It faces the forest, and only someone who knows the right words can make it turn toward them.

### Is Baba Yaga good or evil?

Both. She eats the rude and the careless, but she helps heroes who are polite, brave or kind, giving them food, advice and magic gifts.

### What does Baba Yaga ride?

She flies in an iron mortar, rowing through the air with a pestle and sweeping away her tracks with a broom.
