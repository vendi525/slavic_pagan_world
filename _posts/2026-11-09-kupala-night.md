It is the shortest night of the year. The sky never gets fully dark. On the riverbank a bonfire roars, girls with flower crowns are singing, and couples run hand in hand and leap through the flames. This is Kupala.

## The name

*Kupala* is usually linked to *kupati*, to bathe, since this is a feast of water as much as of fire. Others connect it to *kupa*, a heap, "heaps of straw or brushwood being used for the bonfires which in Russia, as in many other parts of Europe, are the chief characteristics of these Midsummer festivals" (Ralston).[1] When the church came, the feast fell on the day of St John the Baptist, the great baptiser, and so the old bathing god became Ivan Kupala, John the Bather.

## Fire

The heart of Kupala is the bonfire. In Ruthenia it had to be new fire, "procured from wood by friction," made by the elders while everyone stood in silence. "But as soon as the fire is "churned," the bystanders break forth with joyous songs," and the young people "take hands, and spring in couples through the smoke, if not through the flames, and after that the cattle in their turn are driven through it" (Ralston).[2]

The fire also told your future. "Those who do not loosen their hands while jumping will become husband and wife" (Máchal).[4]

In Little Russia, a stake wrapped in straw was set on fire, and as the flame rose, women threw birch boughs into it, calling out: "May my flax be as tall as this bough!" (Ralston).[2]

## Water

In the morning, girls went to the woods to pick flowers and weave wreaths. Then came the bathing, in the river and in the dew, which was "common to various Slavonic peoples, as also is the habit of washing in dew on the morning of the festival" (Ralston).[3]

The Belarusians sang a song at sunrise on St John's Day: "Ivan and Marya / Bathed on the hill / Where Ivan bathed / The bank shook / Where Marya bathed, / The grass sprouted." A Russian folklorist explained it as Perun and Lada bathing in the springs of heaven: "He shook the earth with his thunderbolts, she made the grass grow in the fields" (Ralston).[3]

## The fern flower

All over the Slavic lands, people said the fern blooms only on Kupala night. Ralston describes how it "bursts into golden or fiery blossoms, but they disappear almost instantaneously, for evil spirits swarm thickly around them." But "whoever can gather these flowers will be able to read the secrets of the earth, and no treasures can be concealed from him" (Ralston).[5] Young people still go looking for it after the bonfire burns low, in pairs, deep into the forest. Few come back with a flower. Many come back engaged.

## The straw figure

Kupala has a dark side, too. A straw figure of Kupalo was dressed in women's clothes, set beside a tree hung with ribbons, carried through the bonfire, and the next day stripped and thrown into the stream. The tree had a name: "Marena [Winter or Death]" (Ralston).[3] "In many parts of the country figures of Kupalo and Marena are made and afterwards drowned and burned like a Rusalka" (Máchal).[4] At the top of the year, the sun begins to die, and the village says goodbye.

## What is old and what is new

Old: midsummer bonfires, jumping through fire, wreaths, dew-bathing, the fern flower and the drowned straw figure, recorded in every Slavic country. A 16th-century Polish writer already complained of girls who lit fires and "sang Satanic songs" on this night (Ralston).[2]

Newer: a god called Kupala is not found in old sources. Kupalo is the name of the feast and of the straw figure. The link between "Ivan and Marya" and Perun and Lada is a 19th-century folklorist's reading.

## Questions about Kupala Night

### What is Kupala Night?

Kupala Night is the Slavic midsummer festival on the night of 23 to 24 June. It celebrates the summer solstice with bonfires, flower wreaths, bathing and fortune-telling.

### What do people do on Kupala Night?

They light bonfires and jump over them in pairs, float flower wreaths on rivers, bathe in rivers and dew, and search the forest for the magical fern flower.

### What is the fern flower?

According to legend, the fern blooms for a moment only on Kupala Night. Whoever finds the fiery flower can see hidden treasures and the secrets of the earth.

### Is Kupala a god?

There is no clear evidence of a god called Kupala. Kupalo is the name of the feast and of the straw figure that was burned or drowned at midsummer.
