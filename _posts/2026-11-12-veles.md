## The name

Veles had many names. In the north he was Volos. In old folk tales he became Vouvel the Giant, Trdoglav (the Hard-headed) or simply Črt, the devil. His name is often linked to old Baltic words for the souls of the dead, and that fits him well. Long before he guarded cattle, he was "originally the god of the dead" (Cross and Sherbowitz-Wetzor).[1]

## The god men swore by

The first time Veles appears in writing, warriors are swearing by him. In 907, the men of Prince Oleg made peace with the Byzantine emperors, and they "swore by their weapons and by their god Perun, as well as by Volos, the god of cattle, and thus confirmed the treaty" (Primary Chronicle).[2]

Perun for the sword, Veles for the herd. In a world where cattle meant wealth, swearing by Veles was swearing by everything you owned.

## The sky and the valley

Every great mythology has its battle between the sky and the deep, and for the Slavs it was Perun against Veles. Veles could change his shape, and "akin to the Baltic Velnias" he was "able to transform himself into a dragon or a snake" (Kropej).[3]

Even the land remembered their war. "Since he represented the underground world, people built him temples in valleys. Temples dedicated to Perun, in contrast, were erected on hills because Perun was the god of the heavens" (Kropej).[3]

Picture it. The thunder god on the mountaintop, the dragon coiled in the valley below. Veles steals Perun's cattle, or his bride, and hides them in his castle of rock. Perun strikes with his axe of lightning. The castle breaks open, the cattle run free, and the rain finally falls.

## The giant in the mountain

The old story lives on in a folk tale about Vouvel the Giant, who keeps a great herd of cattle inside a mountain. One day, while Vouvel is away, a blacksmith finds his door and strikes it with his hammer:

> "A white lady answers from within: "Whoever you may be, you cannot open this door. You must find the vouvelica grass and say: 'Whoa, cow, return to Krsnik, the earth needs rain!' The door shall open quickly then.""
>
> <cite>Kropej[4]</cite>

The vouvelica grass blooms only on Midsummer Night, between midnight and one. The blacksmith searches and searches but never finds it. Then a black cloud rolls over the hills, and in the middle of it rides Vouvel, coming home. The blacksmith runs.

The cattle in the mountain are the rain clouds, locked away. Whoever opens the door brings the rain back to the earth.

## Guardian of the herds

Veles was not only a thief. He was dangerous, but also caring. Even though he could herd cattle into his underwater world, "he watched over cattle herds in the wintertime. People worshipped him as the protector of cattle" (Kropej).[4]

Farmers remembered him at every harvest. In southern Russia people would "tie the last handful of ears into a knot, this being called "plaiting the beard of Veles"" (Máchal).[5] The last of the field was left for the god below it.

When Christianity came, Vladimir had the idols of Veles thrown into the river, and his care for the herds passed to Saint Blaise, the shepherd saint.[5] But Veles never fully left. ## Questions about Veles

### Who is Veles in Slavic mythology?

Veles is the Slavic god of the underworld, cattle and riches, and the eternal rival of Perun, the god of thunder. He could take the shape of a dragon or a snake.

### What is Veles the god of?

Veles is the god of the underworld and the dead, of cattle and herds, and of hidden wealth. He was also linked with magic and healing.

### What is the myth of Perun and Veles?

Veles steals Perun's cattle or bride and hides them in the underworld. Perun strikes him with lightning, the stolen riches are freed, and rain falls on the earth.

### Is Veles evil?

No. Christian writers later turned him into the devil, but Veles was a god of the dark half of the world, dangerous and generous at once. He guarded the herds through the winter.
