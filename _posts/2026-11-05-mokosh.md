In Slavic mythology she is the dark, rain-soaked earth where every seed wakes, and the woman at the spinning wheel whose thread never ends.

## The name

Her name may hold two old words. In Old Church Slavonic, "the root mok- denotes wet, damp, the root mot- denotes to coil, to spin" (Kropej).[1] Water and thread. In Russia people called her Mat syra, Damp Mother Earth.

## The only goddess on the hill

In the year 980, Prince Vladimir of Kyiv raised idols of the Slavic pagan gods on a hill outside his castle: "one of Perun, made of wood with a head of silver and a mustache of gold, and others of Khors, Dazh'bog, Stribog, Simar'gl, and Mokosh'" (Primary Chronicle).[2] Thunder, sun, wind. And one woman.

Why her? Think of what people needed most: rain. No rain meant no crops, no bread, no way through winter. Many old religions honored the forces of nature, and rain was among the most celebrated. Mokosh is the earth that opens to it. Picture Perun, the thunder god, breaking the sky open. The rain falls into her, and she gives birth to the harvest. She was a goddess "of cyclical circulation and renewal, fertility" (Kropej).[1]

So the Slavs had two great female forces. Mokosh gives life. Morana, the goddess of winter, takes it away. Between the two of them, the year turns.

## Goddess of spinning

Mokosh did not only feed the fields. She also sat with the women. She "taught people how to bake bread, spin, weave and heal others" (Kropej).[1] In folk belief she might "come to a house at night time to spin," and people left her small gifts: "a tuft of wool, a sheaf of flax, and doilies" (Kropej).[3]

The spinning wheel and the water have something in common. Neither one stops. The river keeps flowing, the wheel keeps turning, the thread keeps growing. Like time, you cannot hold it still.

And the women who spin also decide your life. The fates who visit a newborn child belong to "this mighty female deity who had power over life and death" (Kropej).[4] Every thread she spins might be someone's life.

## Friday, her day

Long after the idols fell, Mokosh lived on in the days of the week. Friday became the day of Petka, a female spirit who guarded women's work and "punished women who violated the ban on spinning, washing, weaving, etc. on a certain time on Fridays" (Kropej).[5] Even the goddess of work wanted a day of rest. The same rule returns in winter, on {% include wiki-link.html slug="lucija-night" text="Lucija night" %}.

## The witch in the marsh

As people forgot her, Mokosh grew darker. An old legend tells of Mokoška, a heathen princess who lived in a castle in a marsh, surrounded by gardens that were always green:

> "She occasionally helped people but sometimes also harmed them; she was especially wont to taking children with her. At long last, God punished her. On a stormy night, the castle and all its gardens sank into the ground. But Mokoška was not doomed. She continued to appear, disguised in different female forms. She still carries off children, especially those who have been neglected by their parents."
>
> <cite>Kropej[6]</cite>

The castle sank, but she did not. She only changed her face.

## Questions about Mokosh

### Who is Mokosh in Slavic mythology?

Mokosh is the Slavic goddess of damp Mother Earth, water, fertility and spinning. She is the only female deity among the gods of Kyiv recorded in 980.

### What is Mokosh the goddess of?

Mokosh is the goddess of the fertile, rain-soaked earth and of women's work: spinning, weaving and healing. Through the thread she spins, she is also linked to fate, life and death.

### Is Mokosh the goddess of winter?

No. Mokosh is the goddess of earth, fertility and life. The Slavic goddess of winter and death is Morana.

### Why is Friday Mokosh's day?

Mokosh lived on in Petka, the spirit of Friday, who protected women's work and forbade spinning and washing on Fridays.
